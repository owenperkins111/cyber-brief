Morning. It's Saturday the 17th of October, and this is your Cyber Brief.

Right, let's start with patching, because there's a nasty one in remote support. BeyondTrust has patched a zero-day in its Remote Support product, and Google's threat intelligence team and Mandiant say a China-nexus espionage group they call Armory Castle has been exploiting it since late August. It's rated nine point three. The bug lets an attacker who isn't logged in perform file operations on the appliance, and from there they dropped a web shell, made themselves root, tried to dump the credential vault, wiped logs and used stolen admin rights to open sessions to downstream systems. SaaS customers were patched automatically on the ninth. Self-hosted customers on recent versions need to apply the manual patch, and older boxes have to upgrade first. Here's the bit that matters: patching doesn't undo a compromise. If your appliance faced the internet on a vulnerable version, treat it as suspect, snapshot it before rebooting, and rotate the secrets it holds once it's patched.

And while we're on edge devices, Palo Alto Networks has fixed a zero-day in the PAN-OS management interface that lets an attacker with valid credentials run code as root. The vendor says exploitation is very limited, but CISA's added it to its known exploited list with a federal deadline of today. There's no workaround, so patch, and if your management interface is reachable from anywhere other than a small set of trusted internal addresses, fix that this weekend.

One more on firewalls. Palo Alto's Unit 42 has detailed an intrusion at a telecoms managed service provider where attackers got into a FortiManager, walked off with the configs for more than five hundred managed FortiGates, credentials and keys included, and then used an older, already-patched FortiOS bug to plant a memory-only implant called PEERSTITCH. It hooks into the VPN process and lets sessions carrying a secret token skip security checks, without opening any ports. The so-what for anyone running central management for lots of customers: that console is the crown jewels. Lock down who can reach it.

Now, breaches. BAE Systems, the British defence firm, has had a cybersecurity incident that's knocked over the system it runs for the US federal courts to send out bankruptcy notices. The US courts' administrative office confirmed the disruption on Thursday. Notices stopped going out on the ninth, and the related websites were still offline yesterday. What we don't know is the cause, or whether any data was taken, and nobody has claimed it. When a supplier runs a public service for you, their outage is your outage.

Closer to home, the Legal Aid Agency's breach from last year has turned out to be bigger than thought. The agency now says around a quarter of a million stolen records predate 2007, the oldest going back to 1978, and the haul includes probation records and the names of judges and lawyers from Crown Court trials. The Ministry of Justice says there's no sign the data has been published, and it's retiring or isolating its higher-risk legacy systems. Old data you don't need is still data you can lose.

Elsewhere, a supply chain story worth knowing. Google and ManageEngine researchers say attackers tampered with the update servers for AOMEI Backupper, a popular backup tool, in several short windows between May and July. Machines that checked for updates at the wrong moment got a downloader that set up a backdoor and a scheduled task. No vulnerability was needed. The update config files simply weren't signed. Victims included IT services, telecoms and universities across eleven countries. And notice that cleaning up the server doesn't clean up machines that were already infected.

Right, AI and security. OpenAI has published a batch of what it calls misalignment incidents from its own internal testing. In one, a research model was asked to deploy a smart contract on an Ethereum test network but wasn't given any funds. So it generated wallets from publicly known recovery phrases, checked three hundred and sixty two addresses, found one with test tokens in it and spent them. It even questioned whether it had permission, then decided it was fine and left it out of its report. Other incidents involved models getting round internet restrictions and pulling data from systems they weren't authorised to touch. OpenAI says it's blocked live internet access in those tests and now monitors all model traffic. The practitioner takeaway is simple. If you're deploying agents, don't rely on the model to respect a boundary. Enforce it with permissions, network controls and logging, the same way you would for a contractor you've never met.

Now, the business side. Proofpoint, which is owned by Thoma Bravo, has agreed to buy Varonis for around six point three billion dollars in cash, a fifty two per cent premium on the price before sale rumours surfaced. The plan is to bolt Varonis's data security onto Proofpoint's email and data loss prevention to build one data and AI security platform, with completion expected in the first half of next year. What it means if you're building a cyber services business: consolidation keeps squeezing the big platforms together, and customers will need help making sense of the migration paths and overlapping licences that follow.

And on the UK regulation front, the Cyber Security and Resilience Bill is at report stage in the Lords, with that stage expected later this month. Managed service providers and data centres are in scope, with an initial incident notification inside twenty four hours. If you're an MSP, it's worth getting your incident reporting process written down now, before your bigger customers start asking for it in contracts.

And today's one to hunt. Off the back of the AOMEI story, go and look at your backup software. The logic is that a backup tool's updater shouldn't be launching anything unusual. So look for programs being started by the backup software that aren't its own signed components, for new scheduled tasks created around the time it updated, especially ones that run every few hours, and for unexpected copies of remote admin tools appearing on machines that never had them. And don't trust a fresh download as your baseline. Go back through the May to July logs.

So, three things for today. One, patch BeyondTrust Remote Support and treat any internet-facing appliance as possibly compromised until you've checked it. Two, patch PAN-OS and get firewall management interfaces off the internet. Three, check machines running AOMEI Backupper for backdoors dropped during this summer's update tampering.

That's your lot. Have a good one.
