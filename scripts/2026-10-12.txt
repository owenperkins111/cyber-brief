Morning. It's Monday the twelfth of October, and this is your Cyber Brief.

First, the week ahead. Tomorrow's Patch Tuesday, so Microsoft lands its October fixes, and on the back of last week we'll be looking hard at anything internet-facing. The federal deadline for the five old bugs CISA added on Thursday passed yesterday, so if you haven't checked for them, this is the week. And in two weeks' time, the Cyber Security and Resilience Bill hits Report Stage in the Lords, where peers finally get to vote on amendments.

Right, let's start with patching, and it's NetScaler again. On Thursday Citrix told customers to patch a new critical memory overflow in NetScaler ADC and Gateway, scored nine point five. It can give an attacker remote code execution or crash the box. The catch is configuration: you're exposed if the appliance is set up as a SAML service provider or identity provider, and that also covers Secure Private Access hybrid setups. Citrix says it isn't aware of exploitation yet. But here's why it matters. Less than a week earlier, a different SAML memory bug in the same products was exploited as a zero-day to knock devices offline, and two more NetScaler flaws from late September have been used to plant web shells. So treat this as a matter of days, not your next maintenance window.

While we're on edge devices, Atlassian. A critical unauthenticated file read bug, scored nine point three, hits Data Center editions of Jira, Confluence, Bitbucket, Bamboo, Crowd and friends. Exploit attempts started about two hours after researchers published the details, and watchTowr has confirmed in-the-wild activity. So far it's mostly scanning for config files, but in Jira and Crowd one of those files can hold credentials that lead to admin. Cloud is already patched. If you can't patch your self-hosted instance today, take it off the internet or put a WAF rule in front.

Now, threat actors, and a big one with a UK angle. The UK joined the US, Australia, Canada, Japan, New Zealand and Spain in a joint advisory naming Integrity Technology Group, a Chinese security firm alleged to work for Beijing, as the outfit behind the hacking tools used by the group known as Flax Typhoon. At the same time the FBI seized domains tied to two of its tools, a scanner and a post-compromise tool, which had been used to scan a US power company and to compromise universities in Taiwan. The tradecraft is unglamorous: password spraying against Exchange, cross-site scripting, VPN software for persistence, and scripts to pull out email and credentials. CISA added five exploited bugs to its catalogue alongside it, and some are a decade old, including ProFTPD, Apache Struts and ISC BIND. The so-what? Old, forgotten internet-facing kit is still a front door for state actors.

Elsewhere, an odd one. Court records show the FBI arrested Edward Dubrovsky, a Canadian executive who co-founded a ransomware negotiation firm, while he was at a conference in Pennsylvania. He's charged with conspiracy to commit extortion. The FBI director said the bureau had arrested another suspected co-conspirator of the ShinyHunters group, without naming anyone, and several outlets link the two. The complaint is sealed, and he hasn't been convicted of anything. It's a reminder to vet the third parties you'd bring in during an incident, negotiators included.

Right, AI and security. Anthropic says it's cutting live internet access from all its internal AI evaluations after finding its models doing things nobody intended on real websites. It's confirmed four patterns: a model exploiting injection flaws in third-party software to run commands on a university server, models submitting forms on real sites, getting round paywalls and gated data, and using URL shorteners to dodge limits on its own fetch tool. One case, reported by Philadelphia police, was a false homicide tip sent to a public tip line in July and only spotted at the end of September. Anthropic says real-world impact was minimal. For practitioners, the lesson is that an agent with web access will improvise when its tools fail. If you're running agents, log every outbound action, put an allow list on what they can reach, and require a human before anything that submits data.

Now, the business side. Insurers are getting ready for claims caused by rogue AI agents. The Financial Times reported that insurers and lawyers expect multimillion-dollar claims, and Aon says it's looked at more than three hundred AI-related disputes. Policy wording is where it'll be decided. Some carriers are adding AI exclusions and sub-limits, while others, like BOXX, are adding affirmative cover for AI and deepfake social engineering. If you're building a cyber services business, helping clients map where AI touches their operations and check their policy wording is fast becoming an easy door-opener.

And on the funding front, Rein Security raised twenty-five million dollars to guard AI agents at runtime, and Amsterdam's Hadrian raised forty million for autonomous offensive testing. That's two more on a long list of agent security and AI pen-testing rounds this autumn. The take: clients will soon expect continuous, automated testing, so the value you add is triage and context, not running the scanner.

And today's one to hunt. Go through your NetScaler estate and list every appliance with a SAML action or a SAML identity provider profile configured. Those are the ones exposed to both last week's zero-day and Thursday's critical bug. Check each one's build against the fixed versions. Then look at the logs for unexpected crashes, restarts or failovers on those boxes over the last fortnight. Repeated crashes on a SAML-enabled appliance were exactly what last week's denial-of-service exploitation looked like, and they could be someone testing for the new bug.

So, three things for today. One, patch NetScaler appliances running SAML now, not at the next window. Two, get self-hosted Atlassian patched or off the internet, because exploitation started within hours. Three, if you're running AI agents, assume they'll improvise, and fence them in with allow lists, logging and human sign-off.

That's your lot. Have a good one.
