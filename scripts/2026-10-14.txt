Morning. It's Wednesday the 14th of October, and this is your Cyber Brief.

Right, let's start with Patch Tuesday, because it was a monster. Microsoft fixed somewhere north of fourteen hundred vulnerabilities last night, depending on whose count you use. That's a record, and more than it published in the whole of 2023. Only one is confirmed as exploited in the wild. It's a Windows Shell bug that gets around Mark of the Web, the warning that's meant to stop a downloaded file running quietly. Someone sends a booby-trapped file, the user opens it, and the usual protections don't kick in. CISA added it to its known exploited list the same day. Patch that one first. Next, a .NET Framework flaw that can sidestep Windows Defender Application Control was publicly disclosed before the fix, which gives attackers a head start. After that, look at what's exposed. There are critical, potentially wormable bugs in DHCP Server, one rated nine point eight, plus Remote Desktop Gateway, Network Policy Server and Active Directory Domain Services. And on-prem Exchange gets a critical remote code execution fix triggered by a malicious email. One more thing. For Windows 11 Home and Pro on 24H2, this was the last update. Sort those machines out.

And while we're on edge devices, Ivanti. Volexity has found an attacker chaining three zero-days in Ivanti Connect Secure to get root on the VPN appliance without logging in, and it reportedly worked on a box that was fully patched at the time. Exploitation started on the third of October. Ivanti confirms limited exploitation and has fixed two of the three bugs. The privilege escalation piece is still waiting for a patch in the coming weeks. Here's the nasty part. The attacker left no web shell. They just re-ran the whole chain for every command, and Ivanti's own Integrity Checker doesn't spot it. Once in, they pulled stored credentials, including the LDAP account the appliance uses to talk to Active Directory, then went password spraying. Volexity thinks, with low confidence, it's criminal rather than espionage. So upgrade now, but don't treat the upgrade as the end of it. If you've had one of these exposed this month, assume those credentials are burned and rotate them.

Quick one on Fortinet as well. It's patched a critical authentication bypass in FortiSandbox, rated nine point six, that lets an unauthenticated attacker run commands through the web interface. No exploitation reported yet, but Fortinet kit has a habit of getting attention quickly, so get it on this week's list.

Now, breaches and incidents. TFG London, the UK arm of the South African retailer behind Phase Eight, Hobbs and Whistles, has confirmed a cyber incident. It spotted it on Sunday. Some IT systems and website functions are affected, stores are open, and orders placed since Sunday may be delayed. It's brought in outside specialists and told the authorities, and the Information Commissioner's Office says it's assessing. The company says it has no reason to believe payment information is at risk. What it hasn't said is whether customer data was taken, and nobody has publicly claimed the attack. That makes it the latest in a run of fashion names, after ASOS confirmed a breach and H&M began looking into claims about its data. With Black Friday a few weeks out, retail is clearly in the crosshairs. If you look after retailers, now's the time to check help desk verification and third-party access.

Elsewhere, Cisco Talos has flagged ClickFix campaigns hiding behind trusted services. In one, aimed at crypto traders, the victim is talked into pasting JavaScript into their browser, which then pulls its real payload from a public Google spreadsheet. Another used fake Google verification prompts on a compromised site to drop the Amatera info stealer. The common thread? The user runs the attack themselves, and the traffic goes to domains your filters trust. Worth a line in your next awareness session: real verification never asks you to paste and run anything.

Right, AI and security. Why is Patch Tuesday suddenly so huge? Microsoft says it's using AI to find and fix bugs in its own code, and researchers are doing the same. Dustin Childs at the Zero Day Initiative puts it nicely. AI isn't creating these bugs out of thin air, it's finding them sooner. The good news is exploitation hasn't risen at the same pace, yet. The bad news is monthly patch triage is creaking. Microsoft's even trialling new risk cohorts that group related fixes to help teams plan. The practical lesson? Stop sorting by count or severity alone. Lead with what's exploited, what's publicly known and what's internet facing, and accept that you might need to patch faster than once a month.

Now, the business side. Two funding rounds point the same way. Pluto Security came out of stealth with forty-two million dollars to watch the AI tools and agents staff are using, from browser extensions to MCP servers, and it says about sixty percent of the risk it finds comes from vibe-coded tools built by non-developers, mostly in finance, marketing and legal. And Reach Security raised more than fifty million in a Series B for AI that checks whether your existing security tools are actually configured to block known attack paths, and fixes them when they drift. The take for anyone building a cyber services business: clients don't want another product. They want someone to tell them what their AI is doing, and whether the controls they've already paid for actually work. Both are services you can sell today.

And today's one to hunt. Tied to the Ivanti chain. Because the attacker steals the appliance's own LDAP credentials and then sprays passwords, the place to catch them is your domain, not the appliance. Take the IP address of your VPN appliance and the service account it uses, and look for that account or that address doing anything unusual, like failed logons against lots of different users, or logons to machines that aren't domain controllers. A VPN box should talk to Active Directory in a very boring, predictable way. Anything noisy is worth a look.

So, three things for today. One, push out Patch Tuesday, starting with the exploited Windows Shell bug, then exposed DHCP, Remote Desktop Gateway and Exchange servers. Two, upgrade Ivanti Connect Secure, rotate the credentials stored on it, and hunt for password spraying from the appliance. Three, if you work with retailers, tighten help desk and third-party access checks before the Black Friday rush.

That's your lot. Have a good one.
